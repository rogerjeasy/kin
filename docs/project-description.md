# Kin

**Nothing about Mom's money without Mom.**

Product description. PayPal AI Hackathon 2026. Draft 1, 13 October 2026.

---

## 1. Summary

Kin is a shared money layer for families who look after an aging parent. Siblings pay into one family pool, an AI agent pays the parent's bills, caregivers and regular purchases from it, and Kin records who gave what and settles imbalances, so no one quietly carries the cost alone.

**In one sentence:** Kin lets a family share the cost of caring for a parent fairly and openly, while the parent keeps a voice in every decision about their own money.

**What is new:**

1. **Fairness as a payout.** The family agrees how costs are shared, and Kin closes the gap between that agreement and reality with real payments, each with a receipt and an approval.
2. **Payment events as speech.** Each confirmed PayPal event becomes a plain spoken sentence, and each decision the parent makes is a spoken command Kin confirms, so a parent who cannot read a screen can still run their own affairs (section 6.6).

---

## 2. The problem

Families run the money side of a parent's care on group chats, memory, guilt and one sibling's credit card.

### 2.1 The cost lands on one person, and nobody can see it

The sibling who lives closest usually pays for the pharmacy, the plumber, the groceries and the caregiver, meaning to ask the others later. Often they never do, or it becomes an argument.

- About 63 million Americans are family caregivers, nearly one adult in four (AARP and National Alliance for Caregiving, 2025).
- Family caregivers spend on average $7,242 a year out of pocket, about a quarter of their income (AARP, 2021).
- Their unpaid care was valued at $600 billion a year in the United States alone (AARP, 2023).

Time goes unrecorded too: the sibling who drives to appointments gets no credit, and the one far away has no clear way to help. Resentment grows on both sides, and it is often a cause of lasting rifts between adult siblings.

### 2.2 The parent is left out of their own finances

Once the children take over the bills, decisions are made about the parent, not with them. After sixty years of running a household, that is a real loss of dignity.

The barrier is often physical, not mental. Vision loss rises steeply with age, and most of the 2.2 billion people worldwide with a vision impairment are over 50 (WHO). Payment apps, statements and bills all assume good eyesight, so a parent fully able to decide is shut out.

### 2.3 Caregivers are paid late and informally

Aides, cleaners, drivers and visiting nurses are often paid late, in cash, by whichever sibling remembers, with no record of hours, no receipt and no proof of income. A good caregiver paid unreliably leaves, and the arrangement collapses.

### 2.4 Older adults are the main target of payment fraud

Americans aged 60 and over reported nearly $4.9 billion in fraud losses in 2024, a sharp rise on the year before (FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center). The usual scam is a call or letter about an overdue bill, and a parent unsure which bills are paid cannot tell a real demand from a false one.

### 2.5 Today's tools each solve one slice

| What families use today | What it does well |
|---|---|
| Expense-splitting apps | Tracks who owes whom |
| A joint bank account or shared card | One place for the money |
| Care-coordination apps | Schedules, updates, tasks |
| Fraud-monitoring services for seniors | Alerts on unusual activity |
| A spreadsheet and a group chat | Free and flexible |

Nothing combines the shared pool, the fair split, the caregiver's pay and the parent's own voice. Kin does.

## 3. Who Kin is for

Kin serves a **family circle**: the people who share responsibility for one older person's wellbeing and money.

### 3.1 The people in a circle

| Person | Who they are | What they need |
|---|---|---|
| **The parent** | Rose, 81, at home. Sharp, but macular degeneration means she can no longer read her statements. | To know what is paid for her, decide what is hers, and feel safe when the phone rings. |
| **The organizer** | Elena, 54, twenty minutes away. Handles almost everything and has paid for most of it. | To stop being the family bank and bookkeeper, and be reimbursed without asking. |
| **The distant sibling** | Marcus, 51, in another country. Earns well and feels guilty. | A fair way to do his part, and proof of where the money goes. |
| **The stretched sibling** | Ana, 47, nearby. Less money, a lot of time. | For her hours to count, and a share she can afford. |
| **The caregiver** | Amara, a home aide who visits Rose three mornings a week. | To be paid on time, every time, with a record of her hours and income. |

### 3.2 The first audience

Two to four adult siblings aged roughly 40 to 65, at least one in another city, with a parent still at home and at least one paid caregiver or regular service. Here the pain is sharpest: enough money to cause conflict, enough distance for mistrust, and a parent still able to take part.

### 3.3 The wider audience

- Only children and spouses who carry it alone and need the record more than the split.
- Families across countries, where one branch sends money home for a parent's care.
- Geriatric care managers, daily money managers, elder-law attorneys and court-appointed guardians, who must account for every dollar.
- Home-care agencies that want families to pay, and aides to be paid, reliably.

## 4. Product principles

1. **The parent is a participant, not a subject.** Whatever Kin tells the siblings it can tell the parent, and the parent's decisions are asked of them.
2. **Fairness is agreed once, then measured always.** The family decides what is fair. Kin makes the agreement visible and holds everyone to it.
3. **Every dollar has a receipt and an approval.** Nothing leaves the pool without a record of what it was for and who said yes.
4. **Voice first, screen second.** The parent and the caregiver mostly talk to Kin; screens are for whoever wants them.
5. **The AI proposes, people approve, the record decides.** A person confirms every movement of money, and what happened is taken from PayPal's records, never from the AI (section 8).

## 5. A month with Kin

**Day 1.** Elena sets up a circle for her mother, Rose, with Marcus and Ana. They agree on shares (Marcus 50 percent, Elena 30, Ana 20), count care hours at a rate they pick together, and each set up a monthly contribution. Rose joins as "decides": any purchase above $50 needs her spoken yes.

**Day 3.** Rose's phone rings. "Good morning Rose. Your electricity bill of 84 dollars was paid from the family pool this morning. Nothing else is due this week." She didn't have to open a thing.

**Day 6.** After her third visit of the week, Amara tells Kin: "Bill the family for this week, three visits, four hours each." Elena approves with one tap, and Amara's phone confirms the payment before she reaches her car.

**Day 9.** Rose's blood pressure medication is due. "Your refill is ready to order. It costs 64 dollars. Shall I go ahead?" She says yes, and Kin orders it and tells her when it will arrive.

**Day 12.** Rose holds a letter up to her phone. "This is from the water company. It says 61 dollars is due on the 30th. I have added it to the family's list. Elena will see it today."

**Day 15.** A caller tells Rose her gas account is overdue and she must pay 300 dollars today. She asks Kin. "I find no gas bill outstanding. Your gas bill of 57 dollars was paid on the 2nd. Please do not pay this caller. I have told Elena and Marcus." The family knows within seconds.

**Day 20.** The stairlift needs a 900-dollar repair, more than the pool holds. The siblings choose between a one-off top-up by their shares and spreading the cost over several months, Rose agrees, and the repair is booked.

**Day 30.** Elena paid 180 dollars at the pharmacy herself and photographed the receipts. Ana drove Rose to four appointments. Kin works out that Elena is owed 126 dollars and Ana 40, and sends the settlement once approved. Each sibling gets the same one-page statement, and Rose hears in two minutes that everything is in order.

## 6. Features

### 6.1 The family circle

- One circle per person cared for; two parents in different homes can run two.
- Roles: organizer, contributor, parent, caregiver and observer. An observer, such as a family lawyer, sees everything and changes nothing.
- The parent's role is chosen with them. *Decides*: their spoken approval is needed above a threshold the family sets. *Informed*: they hear everything and can ask anything, and the family approves. *Represented*: a named person with legal authority acts for them, and Kin records that authority.
- A written family agreement sets shares, thresholds, approvers and the value of care hours. Kin drafts it in plain language from a short conversation; every member accepts it.

### 6.2 The pool and contributions

- **One family pool.** In production, a PayPal account held for the family under PayPal's licences, through its partner platform (or a licensed partner), used by Kin only with the family's consent. In the hackathon sandbox, all circles' top-ups land in one PayPal business account held by Kin's app, and Kin's ledger attributes each amount to its circle. Kin has no bank account of its own and takes no custody beyond that (`architecture.md` decision 70).
- Monthly contributions follow each sibling's share and can be paused or changed in everyone's view.
- An unexpected cost brings a one-off top-up request in proportion to each share.
- Out-of-pocket receipts are photographed, then read, categorized and credited by Kin.
- If the family agrees, care hours are credited alongside money at its chosen rate.
- A large one-off cost can be paid over time.

### 6.3 The fairness ledger

- Each sibling can always see what they have given, in money and time, against the agreement.
- Balances come with reasons: "You are 126 dollars ahead this month because you paid three pharmacy bills directly."
- Each month Kin proposes the smallest set of payments that restores the agreed shares, and sends them once approved.
- Every line carries its receipt, approval and payment record, and any sibling can question it.
- **Quiet shares.** For a cost too big for the pool, like Rose's $900 stairlift, nobody has to say aloud what they cannot afford. Each person privately tells their own Kin what they can give ("honestly I can only manage 60, more after my December bonus, and I can drive Mum to her appointments"), then confirms and seals the offer Kin makes of it; only they can read it. Plain code splits the cost by the agreed shares, never above anyone's offer, spreading the rest over those with room. Each person sees their part and the total, never another's limit. If offers fall short, Kin says only by how much, and offers three months to pay or PayPal Pay Later for one person to take the gap. The record still shows what everyone gave. With only two people sharing, Kin says plainly that privacy cannot be total.

### 6.4 Bills and caregiver pay

- Bills arrive by photo, forwarded email or from a caregiver. Kin lists each with its amount and due date.
- Small recurring bills from approved billers are paid automatically; anything new or large waits for a person.
- Kin flags duplicates, unexplained jumps, unfamiliar billers and hours that clash with the visit schedule.

### 6.5 The purchasing agent

- Medication refills, incontinence supplies, groceries and other regular needs are ordered on schedule within a monthly budget: approved categories, approved sellers, approved limits.
- The parent or the family, as the circle's rules say, confirms each purchase before money moves.
- Kin tracks each delivery to the door and tells the parent when to expect it.

### 6.6 The parent's voice

This part of Kin belongs to the parent.

- Confirmed payments, purchases, contributions and deliveries are announced in short, calm sentences, as they happen, daily or weekly, as the parent chooses.
- On their own decisions the parent says yes, no or "ask me later", and Kin repeats the answer before acting.
- They can ask anything ("What did we spend on medicine this month?" "Has Amara been paid?" "Who paid for the plumber?") and hear the answer from the record.
- **AI eyes.** Any letter, bill or receipt held up to the camera is read aloud, explained and, if needed, added to the family's list.
- **Scam check.** Faced with a demand for money by phone, letter or message, the parent asks Kin, which checks it against what was really billed and paid, says plainly whether it is real, and alerts the family.
- Kin always introduces itself the same way and never asks for a password, card number or code. The family teaches one rule: Kin tells, Kin never asks for secrets.
- A two-minute spoken statement each month: what came in, what went out, what is coming.
- **The parent's Purse.** An allowance of her own; the family sets a weekly amount, a ceiling and a per-payment limit. She says "Send Lily 25 dollars for her birthday", Kin reads it back, and her yes alone sends it. A payment waits a day if it goes outside the address book the family checked, is in gift cards or crypto, is over her limit, or sounds pressured. Kin gently asks whether someone is telling her what to do, and the family is told and can stop it. The family sees the ceiling, balance and monthly total, not her payees or reasons unless she shares them, and she sees exactly what they see. A coerced instruction can cost at most what is in the Purse.

### 6.7 The caregiver's side

- The caregiver says the visits and hours. Kin drafts the bill and reads it back, the organizer approves it, and payment is sent and confirmed the same day.
- Every visit billed and payment received is kept as proof of income.
- Purchases for the parent are reimbursed with their pay, from a photographed receipt.
- They see only their own bills and payments.

### 6.8 Statements and audit

- A one-page monthly statement, identical for every sibling: contributions, spending by category, caregiver pay, settlement and what to expect next month.
- A year in review by category and person, for tax time and for talking about what comes next.
- An audit export of every movement, dated, with its receipt and approval, to the standard a court expects of someone managing another person's money.
- Nothing is ever deleted. Corrections are new entries.

### 6.9 Safeguards

- Money goes only to approved billers, approved caregivers and circle members. Above the agreement's limits, or a threshold the family sets, two members must approve, so no one can drain the pool.
- New billers, changed shares and new members are announced to the whole circle.
- Any member can freeze outgoing payments for 24 hours if something looks wrong.
- Unusual patterns, such as a sudden new payee or repeated requests to the parent, are flagged to the whole circle, not to one person.
- **Her wishes, in her own words.** While she can decide, the parent says what should happen to her money if she goes into hospital or a care home ("Always keep paying Amara, even if I'm in hospital"). Kin reads each wish back as a plain rule; she confirms it and two of the family witness it. When two people (or one of the family and her caregiver) say she is in hospital, Kin acts on her wishes as she said them: her caregiver stays paid, extras wait, subscriptions pause, and a new payee needs the people she named. Each decision made for her cites her, with her words one tap away. She can change them while she can decide, not while she is away. They complement a power of attorney and an advance directive and do not replace them.

### 6.9a Connected assistants and agencies

- A member connects the AI assistant they already use (Claude, ChatGPT or any other that speaks MCP) on the Connected assistants screen. It reads the circle's verified record as that member sees it and, if allowed, may propose a refill. It can never approve, pay or sign; those wait for a person in Kin.
- Access is scoped, expiring and revocable: read-only or read-and-propose, for as long as the member picks. Every call is listed, refusals included; revoking refuses the very next call.
- An agency integrates once: its software reports aides' visits with a signed key the organizer creates. Reports appear beside the shift on the family's calendar as the agency's word. They pay nothing, and the family still confirms the visit.
- In the demo, Marcus's assistant has read what he owes, tried to approve and been refused; the live console plays the same session for real, in the browser.

### 6.10 Accessibility and language

- Built for low vision and blindness first: the parent can do everything by voice, and the screen has very large type, high contrast and full screen-reader support.
- Large touch targets, adjustable speaking speed and repeat on request help unsteady hands and slow hearing.
- Each member uses their own language: a caregiver bills in Spanish, the parent listens in Portuguese, the siblings read in English.
- Any ordinary phone will do.

## 7. What PayPal does in Kin

PayPal moves every dollar in Kin, and Kin treats PayPal's record as the truth.

**Ran in the sandbox**: Kin's own code did it against the PayPal sandbox and the result is recorded (`packages/paypal/src/fixtures/README.md`, `architecture.md` section 23). **Built, not yet run in the sandbox**: code and tests exist, no sandbox run is recorded. **Planned**: not built.

| Family need | PayPal capability | In this build |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly shares | Subscriptions | Built, not yet run in the sandbox through Kin's screens; a check script had one approved and billed on 14 October 2026. Demo shares are simulated. |
| One-off top-ups | Checkout (Orders v2 with the JavaScript SDK v6) | Ran in the sandbox: first top-up on 16 October 2026 ($100.00, a $3.98 fee). Hosted-demo top-ups are real sandbox checkouts. |
| Spreading a big repair over time | Pay Later | Built into the top-up sheet where PayPal offers it. Not yet run: the sandbox did not offer it to Kin's test buyer. |
| Asking a sibling for a top-up or settlement | Invoicing | Ran in the sandbox: a $166.00 invoice made, sent, paid and read back on 8 November 2026. Demo invoice requests are simulated unless Kin's own sandbox recipient is configured. |
| Buying what the parent needs, after a person confirms | Saved payment method at a linked merchant | Built, not yet run in the sandbox through Kin; a check script proved a saved-wallet charge with nobody signing in on 14 October 2026. Demo refills come from a stand-in pharmacy with simulated money, and a real circle cannot raise one yet (`architecture.md` decision 62). |
| Paying the caregiver the same day | Payouts | Ran in the sandbox: a $1.00 payout read back `SUCCESS` with a $0.25 fee on 26 October 2026. Demo payouts are simulated, or real sandbox payouts to Kin's own test account if its sandbox payee is configured (decision 73). |
| Making whole a sibling who overpaid | Payouts | Built, on the same Payouts call. Always simulated in a demo circle; not yet run against the sandbox for a real circle. |
| Knowing what truly happened | Webhooks, and orders, captures and payouts read back from PayPal | Ran in the sandbox (16 October 2026): the first top-up was recorded from the order read back, the verified webhook found it already recorded, and a forged webhook was refused. |
| Reconciling the Purse each night | Transaction Search | Built, not yet run in the sandbox (its reporting answered 403); the test reply is shaped from PayPal's documentation. |
| Reflecting a refunded or reversed top-up | Refund and reversal notifications (Payments v2) | Built, not yet run in the sandbox; test replies are shaped from PayPal's documentation (decision 97). |
| Following a delivery to the door | Shipment tracking | Planned. The demo follows the stand-in seller's own delivery reports. |
| Contesting a wrong or disputed charge | Disputes | Planned. |
| Confirming who each member is | Log in with PayPal | Planned. Sign-in today is Amazon Cognito. |
| The agent reading PayPal's record for the family | PayPal's agent toolkit and MCP server | Planned. Ask Kin reads PayPal through Kin's own adapter; Kin's own MCP server (decision 93) serves members' assistants and is not PayPal's. |

So the family needs no new bank account or card, and what the parent hears comes from confirmed PayPal events (in the demo, from simulated entries labelled so), which is why she can trust it and why the scam check works. Every PayPal call Kin makes goes to the sandbox with test money, never to live PayPal.

## 8. What AI does in Kin, and what it does not

**AI does the work that needs understanding.** It reads bills, letters and receipts from a photo. It turns speech into a bill, an approval or a question, and payment events into clear speech in the right language. It answers questions from the record, explains the fairness ledger, and spots what looks wrong: duplicate bills, unusual amounts, unfamiliar payees, demands that match nothing on record. It drafts the family agreement, the monthly statement and the audit summary, and plans recurring purchases within the family's rules. It turns what one person privately says they can give into an offer they confirm, never seeing anyone else's offer and never sharing a cost out (section 6.3).

**AI does not do the work that needs certainty.** Ledger and settlement arithmetic is exact and the same every time. PayPal's record decides that a payment happened. A person confirms every movement of money. And the AI gives no legal, tax, medical or investment advice.

The split is deliberate: it is what lets a family trust an AI with a parent's money.

## 9. The value for each person

With Kin, the parent hears everything, decides what is theirs and has a trusted way to check any demand. The organizer pays from the pool instead of fronting costs, is reimbursed automatically and keeps nothing by hand. The distant sibling sees what his share paid for, and the stretched sibling's care hours count beside everyone else's money. The caregiver is paid the same day, not late in cash, with an income history. And money is settled every month instead of going unmentioned until it explodes.

## 10. Why Kin is different

- It starts from the parent. Other tools for older people's money are built for the children or for a monitoring service. Kin is the only one where the older person is the primary user of a voice interface designed for them.
- It moves the money, where splitting apps only say who owes whom (sections 6.2 to 6.4).
- It counts time as well as money, the real source of most family conflict, which other tools ignore.
- Its record stands up to a lawyer, an accountant or a court (section 6.8).
- Its fraud check rests on facts: whether the bill exists and has been paid, not whether a caller sounds suspicious.

## 11. Market and business model

### 11.1 The market

- **United States.** About 63 million family caregivers, each spending over $7,000 a year out of pocket (section 2.1). Even a small share of that through Kin is a large payment volume.
- **Worldwide.** By 2030 one person in six will be 60 or over, reaching 2.1 billion by 2050 (WHO). Families are smaller and more scattered than in any earlier generation, so fewer children share each parent's care, often across borders.
- **Adjacent markets.** Professional fiduciaries and guardians, home-care agencies, and employers who offer caregiving support as a staff benefit.

### 11.2 How Kin earns money

Proposals, to be tested with real families.

| Plan | Who pays | What they get | Proposed price |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Family** | The circle, split like any other cost | Everything in this document for one circle | A flat monthly fee per circle, in the range of a streaming subscription |
| **Parent and caregiver** | Nobody | Full use of their side of Kin | Free, always |
| **Professional** | Care managers, attorneys, guardians | Many circles, client reporting, court-ready exports | Per client, per month |
| **Agency** | Home-care agencies | Billing and same-day pay for their aides across many families | Per active aide |
| **Employer benefit** | Employers | Kin offered to staff who are caregivers | Per enrolled employee |

The family plan is paid by a PayPal subscription from the pool, so Kin's own price is shared by the same fair split as everything else.

### 11.3 Why this is good business for PayPal

Kin brings whole families into PayPal at once, including an older generation and a caregiver workforce that payment apps reach poorly. It brings recurring, predictable volume from subscriptions, invoices and payouts, and shows AI agents doing more valuable work than shopping: carrying a real family responsibility safely.

## 12. Trust, safety and privacy

Kin handles a vulnerable person's money, so trust is the product.

- A circle is active only once the parent, or whoever has legal authority to act for them, agrees to it and to their role.
- The parent's role can change over time, by the circle's agreement, on the record.
- Kin records a power of attorney or guardianship where one exists, and grants authority to no one.
- Financial abuse of older people is most often committed by someone close to them, so the safeguards in section 6.9 cover family as well as strangers.
- Kin keeps financial records and receipts, not medical records, diagnoses or clinical notes.
- The parent, or their representative, can see everything held about them and close the circle.
- Kin is not a bank, a law firm or a care provider, and gives no legal, tax or medical advice. Section 6.2 says how money is held.
- When unsure what a document says or whether a demand is real, Kin says so and asks a person.

## 13. Impact, and how it will be measured

**The aim:** families stop fighting about money while caring for a parent, the parent keeps a say over their own affairs, and paid helpers are paid properly.

| What we measure | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Share of circles where every sibling contributes each month | The burden is really shared |
| Average gap between agreed and actual shares before each settlement | Fairness is improving, not just being reported |
| Hours per month the organizer spends on money tasks | The bookkeeping burden has lifted |
| Share of parent-level decisions answered by the parent | The parent is truly taking part |
| Days from a caregiver's visit to their payment | Caregivers are paid on time |
| Suspicious demands checked, and money not lost | The fraud protection works |
| Families who say money caused less conflict after three months | The outcome that matters most |

## 14. What the hackathon version shows

The submission shows one month for one family. Each visitor to the hosted demo gets a copy of Rose's circle whose history is simulated and labelled "Simulated"; a top-up the visitor makes is a real PayPal sandbox checkout, labelled "PayPal sandbox". Section 7 says which PayPal parts have run in the sandbox.

1. A circle with agreed shares and monthly contributions. The demo starts from Rose's circle already set up, with simulated monthly shares; a person who signs in can start a real circle.
2. The parent hears a bill being paid, on her Listen screen.
3. The caregiver says or types her visits, Claude reads them into days, the server values them, and once approved she is paid. In the demo that payout is simulated unless Kin's own sandbox payee is configured.
4. The parent approves a refill by voice, and it is bought from a stand-in pharmacy with simulated money.
5. The parent holds up a letter and hears it read.
6. Plain code checks a false payment demand against the record and catches it.
7. A sibling's out-of-pocket receipts (typed in; photographing a receipt is not built) are credited, and the money core proposes the month's settlement. In the demo its payouts are simulated and labelled so.
8. The family statement and the audit export are produced, and the export can be checked offline with `kin-verify`.

**The story in the video** follows Rose, Elena, Marcus, Ana and Amara through those eight moments in under three minutes, opening on Rose's voice, not a dashboard.

**How it answers the judging criteria:**

- **Technological implementation.** Seven PayPal APIs (Orders with the JavaScript SDK v6, Payments, Payouts, Subscriptions, Invoicing, Transaction Search and webhooks), each one essential to the product, and a Claude agent bounded by deterministic code.
- **Design.** A complete product for five kinds of user, with a voice experience built for the person hardest to serve.
- **Potential impact.** A named, enormous, well-documented problem, and a demo that addresses each part of it.
- **Innovation.** Fairness settled by payout and payment events delivered as speech, neither found in current products.
- **Presentation.** One family, one month, one clear before and after.

## 15. Roadmap

- **Next.** Pilot with a few real families. Add the professional plan for care managers and guardians.
- **Then.** Cross-border circles with currency handling for families who send money home. Partnerships with home-care agencies and pharmacies.
- **Later.** A circle that plans ahead: forecasting care costs, preparing the family for the move from home care to residential care, and handing the full record to an executor when the time comes.

## 16. Open questions

These need answers before Kin goes to real families. None blocks the hackathon version.

- **Who legally owns the pool account?** The parent, the organizer or a purpose-made family account; the answer affects tax, liability and PayPal's own account rules.
- **How should care hours be valued?** A family-set rate is simple, but some families will find any rate uncomfortable.
- **What are the tax consequences** of siblings reimbursing each other and of paying a caregiver directly, in each country?
- **How is the parent's consent best established and re-confirmed** when capacity is changing?
- **What will families pay?** The pricing in section 11 is a proposal.
